Q: "Here comes a perfectly valid argument: To start with, everyone who is both a rare consumer of Pantene shampoo and an occasional purchaser of Herbal Essences shampoo is a frequent consumer of Tom's of Maine soap or a regular user of Mysore soap. Now, every frequent consumer of Tom's of Maine soap is neither a rare consumer of Pantene shampoo nor an occasional purchaser of Herbal Essences shampoo. We may conclude that everyone who is not both a rare consumer of Pantene shampoo and an occasional purchaser of Herbal Essences shampoo is however a regular user of Mysore soap."
Is the argument, given the explicitly stated premises, deductively valid or invalid?
Options:
- valid
- invalid
A:

To determine the validity of the argument, let's break it down into logical notation:

**1. Define the propositions:**
Let $P$ = "is a rare consumer of Pantene shampoo"
Let $H$ = "is an occasional purchaser of Herbal Essences shampoo"
Let $T$ = "is a frequent consumer of Tom's of Maine soap"
Let $M$ = "is a regular user of Mysore soap"

**2. Translate the premises:**
* **Premise 1:** "Everyone who is both $P$ and $H$ is $T$ or $M$."
* Logic: $(P \land H) \rightarrow (T \lor M)$
* **Premise 2:** "Every $T$ is neither $P$ nor $H$."
* Logic: $T \rightarrow \neg(P \lor H)$
* This is equivalent to: $T \rightarrow (\neg P \land \neg H)$
* Crucially, this implies that if someone is $T$, they cannot be $(P \land H)$. In fact, if someone is $(P \land H)$, they cannot be $T$ (by Modus Tollens).
* So, $(P \land H) \rightarrow \neg T$.

**3. Analyze the logical flow:**
From Premise 1, if a person is $(P \land H)$, they must be $(T \lor M)$.
From Premise 2, if a person is $(P \land H)$, they cannot be $T$.
Therefore, if a person is $(P \land H)$, they *must* be $M$.
* Derived Intermediate Conclusion: $(P \land H) \rightarrow M$.

**4. Translate the conclusion:**
* **Conclusion:** "Everyone who is **not** both $P$ and $H$ is $M$."
* Logic: $\neg(P \land H) \rightarrow M$

**5. Evaluate Validity:**
The argument establishes that if you *are* $(P \land H)$, then you are $M$.
However, the conclusion claims that if you are *not* $(P \land H)$, then you are $M$.

This is a logical error known as **denying the antecedent** (or a confusion regarding the scope of the implication).
* We know: $(P \land H) \rightarrow M$.
* The argument concludes: $\neg(P \land H) \rightarrow M$.

There is no information in the premises about people who are *not* $(P \land H)$. They could be $M$, or they could not be $M$. The premises do not force them to be $M$.

**Example of a counter-model:**
Imagine a person who is neither a rare consumer of Pantene nor an occasional purchaser of Herbal Essences (so $\neg(P \land H)$ is true).
* Premise 1 is satisfied (the condition $P \land H$ is false, so the implication is vacuously true).
* Premise 2 is satisfied (regardless of whether they are $T$ or not, as long as if they are $T$, they aren't $P$ or $H$).
* However, this person does not have to be a regular user of Mysore soap ($M$). They could be neither $T$ nor $M$.
* In this scenario, the premises are true, but the conclusion ("Everyone who is not... is $M$") is false because this specific person is not $M$.

Since it is possible for the premises to be true and the conclusion false, the argument is **invalid**.
