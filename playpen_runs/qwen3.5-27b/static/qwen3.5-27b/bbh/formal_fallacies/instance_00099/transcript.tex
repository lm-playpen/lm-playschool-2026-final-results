                \texttt{To determine the validity of the argument, let's break it down into logical notation:} \\
\\ 
\texttt{**1. Define the propositions:**} \\
\texttt{Let \$P\$ = "is a rare consumer of Pantene shampoo"} \\
\texttt{Let \$H\$ = "is an occasional purchaser of Herbal Essences shampoo"} \\
\texttt{Let \$T\$ = "is a frequent consumer of Tom's of Maine soap"} \\
\texttt{Let \$M\$ = "is a regular user of Mysore soap"} \\
\\ 
\texttt{**2. Translate the premises:**} \\
\texttt{*   **Premise 1:** "Everyone who is both \$P\$ and \$H\$ is \$T\$ or \$M\$."} \\
\texttt{    *   Logic: \$(P \textbackslash{}land H) \textbackslash{}rightarrow (T \textbackslash{}lor M)\$} \\
\texttt{*   **Premise 2:** "Every \$T\$ is neither \$P\$ nor \$H\$."} \\
\texttt{    *   Logic: \$T \textbackslash{}rightarrow \textbackslash{}neg(P \textbackslash{}lor H)\$} \\
\texttt{    *   This is equivalent to: \$T \textbackslash{}rightarrow (\textbackslash{}neg P \textbackslash{}land \textbackslash{}neg H)\$} \\
\texttt{    *   Crucially, this implies that if someone is \$T\$, they cannot be \$(P \textbackslash{}land H)\$. In fact, if someone is \$(P \textbackslash{}land H)\$, they cannot be \$T\$ (by Modus Tollens).} \\
\texttt{    *   So, \$(P \textbackslash{}land H) \textbackslash{}rightarrow \textbackslash{}neg T\$.} \\
\\ 
\texttt{**3. Analyze the logical flow:**} \\
\texttt{From Premise 1, if a person is \$(P \textbackslash{}land H)\$, they must be \$(T \textbackslash{}lor M)\$.} \\
\texttt{From Premise 2, if a person is \$(P \textbackslash{}land H)\$, they cannot be \$T\$.} \\
\texttt{Therefore, if a person is \$(P \textbackslash{}land H)\$, they *must* be \$M\$.} \\
\texttt{*   Derived Intermediate Conclusion: \$(P \textbackslash{}land H) \textbackslash{}rightarrow M\$.} \\
\\ 
\texttt{**4. Translate the conclusion:**} \\
\texttt{*   **Conclusion:** "Everyone who is **not** both \$P\$ and \$H\$ is \$M\$."} \\
\texttt{    *   Logic: \$\textbackslash{}neg(P \textbackslash{}land H) \textbackslash{}rightarrow M\$} \\
\\ 
\texttt{**5. Evaluate Validity:**} \\
\texttt{The argument establishes that if you *are* \$(P \textbackslash{}land H)\$, then you are \$M\$.} \\
\texttt{However, the conclusion claims that if you are *not* \$(P \textbackslash{}land H)\$, then you are \$M\$.} \\
\\ 
\texttt{This is a logical error known as **denying the antecedent** (or a confusion regarding the scope of the implication).} \\
\texttt{*   We know: \$(P \textbackslash{}land H) \textbackslash{}rightarrow M\$.} \\
\texttt{*   The argument concludes: \$\textbackslash{}neg(P \textbackslash{}land H) \textbackslash{}rightarrow M\$.} \\
\\ 
\texttt{There is no information in the premises about people who are *not* \$(P \textbackslash{}land H)\$. They could be \$M\$, or they could not be \$M\$. The premises do not force them to be \$M\$.} \\
\\ 
\texttt{**Example of a counter{-}model:**} \\
\texttt{Imagine a person who is neither a rare consumer of Pantene nor an occasional purchaser of Herbal Essences (so \$\textbackslash{}neg(P \textbackslash{}land H)\$ is true).} \\
\texttt{*   Premise 1 is satisfied (the condition \$P \textbackslash{}land H\$ is false, so the implication is vacuously true).} \\
\texttt{*   Premise 2 is satisfied (regardless of whether they are \$T\$ or not, as long as if they are \$T\$, they aren't \$P\$ or \$H\$).} \\
\texttt{*   However, this person does not have to be a regular user of Mysore soap (\$M\$). They could be neither \$T\$ nor \$M\$.} \\
\texttt{*   In this scenario, the premises are true, but the conclusion ("Everyone who is not... is \$M\$") is false because this specific person is not \$M\$.} \\
\\ 
\texttt{Since it is possible for the premises to be true and the conclusion false, the argument is **invalid**.} \\
\\ 
\texttt{A: invalid} \\
