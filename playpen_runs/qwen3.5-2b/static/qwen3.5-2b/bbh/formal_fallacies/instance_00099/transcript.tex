                \texttt{To determine the validity of the argument, let us break down the logical structure using propositional logic.} \\
\\ 
\texttt{**1. Define the Propositions:**} \\
\texttt{Let's assign variables to the specific conditions mentioned in the text:} \\
\texttt{*   \$P\$: Someone is a rare consumer of Pantene shampoo.} \\
\texttt{*   \$O\$: Someone is an occasional purchaser of Herbal Essences shampoo.} \\
\texttt{*   \$F\$: Someone is a frequent consumer of Tom's of Maine soap.} \\
\texttt{*   \$R\$: Someone is a regular user of Mysore soap.} \\
\\ 
\texttt{**2. Translate the Premises:**} \\
\texttt{*   **Premise 1:** "Everyone who is both a rare consumer of Pantene shampoo and an occasional purchaser of Herbal Essences shampoo is a frequent consumer of Tom's of Maine soap or a regular user of Mysore soap."} \\
\texttt{    *   Logic: \$(P \textbackslash{}land O) \textbackslash{}rightarrow (F \textbackslash{}lor R)\$} \\
\texttt{*   **Premise 2:** "Every frequent consumer of Tom's of Maine soap is neither a rare consumer of Pantene shampoo nor an occasional purchaser of Herbal Essences shampoo."} \\
\texttt{    *   Logic: \$F \textbackslash{}rightarrow \textbackslash{}neg(P \textbackslash{}land O)\$ (This is logically equivalent to saying if \$F\$ is true, then \$P\$ is false AND \$O\$ is false).} \\
\texttt{*   **Conclusion:** "Everyone who is not both a rare consumer of Pantene shampoo and an occasional purchaser of Herbal Essences shampoo is however a regular user of Mysore soap."} \\
\texttt{    *   Logic: \$\textbackslash{}neg(P \textbackslash{}land O) \textbackslash{}rightarrow R\$} \\
\\ 
\texttt{**3. Analyze the Logical Flow:**} \\
\texttt{We need to check if the conclusion follows necessarily from the premises.} \\
\texttt{*   From Premise 2, we know that if \$F\$ is true, then \$\textbackslash{}neg(P \textbackslash{}land O)\$ is true.} \\
\texttt{*   From Premise 1, we know that if \$P \textbackslash{}land O\$ is true, then \$F \textbackslash{}lor R\$ is true.} \\
\texttt{*   Let's look at the contrapositive of Premise 1: \$\textbackslash{}neg(F \textbackslash{}lor R) \textbackslash{}rightarrow \textbackslash{}neg(P \textbackslash{}land O)\$.} \\
\texttt{    *   This means: If someone is neither a frequent consumer of Tom's of Maine soap nor a regular user of Mysore soap, then they are not both a rare consumer of Pantene and an occasional purchaser of Herbal Essences.} \\
\texttt{*   Now, let's look at the conclusion again: \$\textbackslash{}neg(P \textbackslash{}land O) \textbackslash{}rightarrow R\$.} \\
\texttt{    *   This claims that if someone is *not* both \$P\$ and \$O\$, they *must* be \$R\$.} \\
\\ 
\texttt{**4. Identify the Logical Gap (The Fallacy):**} \\
\texttt{The argument commits a formal fallacy known as **Denying the Antecedent** (or a variation thereof depending on the exact structure, but essentially a failure to account for the case where the antecedent of the conclusion is false).} \\
\\ 
\texttt{Let's test this with a counter{-}example:} \\
\texttt{*   Assume a person is **neither** a frequent consumer of Tom's of Maine soap **nor** a regular user of Mysore soap.} \\
\texttt{*   According to Premise 1 (\$ (P \textbackslash{}land O) \textbackslash{}rightarrow (F \textbackslash{}lor R) \$), if they are not in the consequent (\$F \textbackslash{}lor R\$), they cannot be in the antecedent (\$P \textbackslash{}land O\$). Therefore, they are not both \$P\$ and \$O\$.} \\
\texttt{*   According to the Conclusion (\$ \textbackslash{}neg(P \textbackslash{}land O) \textbackslash{}rightarrow R \$), if they are not both \$P\$ and \$O\$, they must be \$R\$.} \\
\texttt{*   **The Contradiction:** The person in this scenario is neither \$R\$ (regular user of Mysore soap) nor \$F\$ (frequent consumer of Tom's of Maine soap).} \\
\texttt{    *   If they are not \$R\$, the conclusion fails.} \\
\texttt{    *   If they are not \$F\$, Premise 2 (\$F \textbackslash{}rightarrow \textbackslash{}neg(P \textbackslash{}land O)\$) tells us nothing about them regarding \$P\$ and \$O\$. They could still be both \$P\$ and \$O\$ (which would make the antecedent of Premise 1 true, forcing them to be \$F\$ or \$R\$, which they aren't).} \\
\\ 
\texttt{Wait, let's re{-}evaluate the counter{-}example more carefully.} \\
\texttt{Let \$X\$ be a person who is **neither** \$F\$ **nor** \$R\$.} \\
\texttt{1.  Is \$X\$ in the antecedent of Premise 1? No, because the antecedent is \$F \textbackslash{}lor R\$. So Premise 1 doesn't tell us anything about \$X\$'s status regarding \$P\$ and \$O\$. \$X\$ could be both \$P\$ and \$O\$.} \\
\texttt{2.  If \$X\$ is both \$P\$ and \$O\$, then by Premise 1, \$X\$ must be \$F\$ or \$R\$. But we defined \$X\$ as neither. This creates a contradiction.} \\
\texttt{3.  Therefore, it is impossible for a person to be both \$P\$ and \$O\$ AND be neither \$F\$ nor \$R\$.} \\
\texttt{4.  So, if a person is neither \$F\$ nor \$R\$, they **cannot** be both \$P\$ and \$O\$. Thus, \$\textbackslash{}neg(P \textbackslash{}land O)\$ is true.} \\
\texttt{5.  The conclusion says: If \$\textbackslash{}neg(P \textbackslash{}land O)\$ is true, then \$R\$ is true.} \\
\texttt{6.  **The Flaw:** The argument assumes that the only way to satisfy the condition \$\textbackslash{}neg(P \textbackslash{}land O)\$ is to be \$R\$. However, as shown in step 3, a person could be neither \$F\$ nor \$R\$ and still satisfy \$\textbackslash{}neg(P \textbackslash{}land O)\$ (by simply not being both \$P\$ and \$O\$).} \\
\texttt{    *   Actually, let's look at the specific case where the conclusion fails.} \\
\texttt{    *   Consider a person who is **neither** \$F\$ **nor** \$R\$.} \\
\texttt{    *   Premise 1: \$(P \textbackslash{}land O) \textbackslash{}rightarrow (F \textbackslash{}lor R)\$. Since \$(F \textbackslash{}lor R)\$ is false, \$(P \textbackslash{}land O)\$ must be false. So \$\textbackslash{}neg(P \textbackslash{}land O)\$ is true.} \\
\texttt{    *   Conclusion: \$\textbackslash{}neg(P \textbackslash{}land O) \textbackslash{}rightarrow R\$. Since \$\textbackslash{}neg(P \textbackslash{}land O)\$ is true, the conclusion requires \$R\$ to be true.} \\
\texttt{    *   But in our hypothetical person, \$R\$ is false.} \\
\texttt{    *   Therefore, the argument is invalid.} \\
\\ 
\texttt{**Conclusion:**} \\
\texttt{The argument fails because it treats the condition "not both \$P\$ and \$O\$" as sufficient to guarantee "being \$R\$", ignoring the possibility that one could be neither \$F\$ nor \$R\$ while still satisfying the condition "not both \$P\$ and \$O\$". This is a classic instance of the fallacy of **Denying the Antecedent** (specifically, denying the antecedent of the conditional in the conclusion based on the antecedent of the premise).} \\
\\ 
\texttt{A: invalid} \\
