Q: "Here comes a perfectly valid argument: To begin with, every close friend of Francine is a cousin of Samantha or an ancestor of Janice or a close friend of Shanika. Moreover, every close friend of Francine is a cousin of Samantha. Finally, being a close friend of Shanika is necessary for being a close friend of Francine. We may conclude: Being an ancestor of Janice is necessary for being a close friend of Francine."
Is the argument, given the explicitly stated premises, deductively valid or invalid?
Options:
- valid
- invalid
A:

To determine the validity of the argument, let's break down the logical structure using propositional logic.

**1. Define the Propositions:**
Let $F$ = "is a close friend of Francine"
Let $S$ = "is a cousin of Samantha"
Let $J$ = "is an ancestor of Janice"
Let $H$ = "is a close friend of Shanika"

**2. Translate the Premises:**
* **Premise 1:** "Every close friend of Francine is a cousin of Samantha or an ancestor of Janice or a close friend of Shanika."
* Logic: $F \rightarrow (S \lor J \lor H)$
* **Premise 2:** "Every close friend of Francine is a cousin of Samantha."
* Logic: $F \rightarrow S$
* **Premise 3:** "Being a close friend of Shanika is necessary for being a close friend of Francine."
* Logic: $F \rightarrow H$ (Note: "A is necessary for B" means $B \rightarrow A$)

**3. Translate the Conclusion:**
* **Conclusion:** "Being an ancestor of Janice is necessary for being a close friend of Francine."
* Logic: $F \rightarrow J$

**4. Analyze the Validity:**
An argument is **deductively valid** if and only if it is impossible for the premises to be true and the conclusion false. In other words, the conclusion must follow necessarily from the premises.

Let's test this with a counter-example (a scenario where premises are true, but the conclusion is false):
* Assume there is a person $x$ who is a close friend of Francine ($F$ is true).
* From Premise 2, $x$ must be a cousin of Samantha ($S$ is true).
* From Premise 3, $x$ must be a close friend of Shanika ($H$ is true).
* From Premise 1, since $S$ and $H$ are true, the disjunction $(S \lor J \lor H)$ is automatically true regardless of the truth value of $J$.
* Now, consider the conclusion: Does $x$ *have* to be an ancestor of Janice ($J$)?
* The premises do not force $J$ to be true. $x$ could be a friend of Francine, a cousin of Samantha, and a friend of Shanika, but **not** an ancestor of Janice.
* If $J$ is false, the conclusion ($F \rightarrow J$) becomes false (True $\rightarrow$ False = False).
* However, all premises remain true in this scenario.

Since we can construct a scenario where the premises are true and the conclusion is false, the argument is **invalid**. The fact that $S$ and $H$ are sufficient to satisfy the condition in Premise 1 means $J$ is not required.
