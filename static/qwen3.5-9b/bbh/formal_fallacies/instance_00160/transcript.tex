\texttt{*   **Premise 1:** "Being a classmate of Williams is necessary for not being a workmate of Leslie."} \\
\texttt{    *   Logical form: If someone is *not* a workmate of Leslie, then they must be a classmate of Williams.} \\
\texttt{    *   \$ \textbackslash{}neg W \textbackslash{}to C \$} \\
\texttt{    *   (Where \$W\$ = workmate of Leslie, \$C\$ = classmate of Williams)} \\

\texttt{*   **Premise 2:** "Being a classmate of Williams is necessary for not being a great{-}grandfather of Joseph."} \\
\texttt{    *   Logical form: If someone is *not* a great{-}grandfather of Joseph, then they must be a classmate of Williams.} \\
\texttt{    *   \$ \textbackslash{}neg G \textbackslash{}to C \$} \\
\texttt{    *   (Where \$G\$ = great{-}grandfather of Joseph)} \\
\\ 
\texttt{*   **Premise 3:** "Every cousin of Tony is a workmate of Leslie and a great{-}grandfather of Joseph."} \\
\texttt{    *   Logical form: If someone is a cousin of Tony (\$T\$), then they are a workmate of Leslie (\$W\$) AND a great{-}grandfather of Joseph (\$G\$).} \\
\texttt{    *   \$ T \textbackslash{}to (W \textbackslash{}land G) \$} \\
\\ 
\texttt{**2. Analyze the Conclusion:**} \\
\\ 
\texttt{*   **Conclusion:** "Everyone who is a cousin of Tony is a classmate of Williams, too."} \\
\texttt{    *   Logical form: If someone is a cousin of Tony (\$T\$), then they are a classmate of Williams (\$C\$).} \\
\texttt{    *   \$ T \textbackslash{}to C \$} \\
\\ 
\texttt{**3. Evaluate the Deduction:**} \\
\\ 
\texttt{Let's test the argument using a hypothetical person who is a **cousin of Tony** (\$T\$ is true).} \\
\\ 
\texttt{*   From **Premise 3**, if \$T\$ is true, then \$(W \textbackslash{}land G)\$ is true. This means the person is both a workmate of Leslie (\$W\$) and a great{-}grandfather of Joseph (\$G\$).} \\
\texttt{*   Now look at **Premise 1** (\$ \textbackslash{}neg W \textbackslash{}to C \$). Since the person *is* a workmate (\$W\$ is true), the antecedent (\$\textbackslash{}neg W\$) is false. In logic, a conditional statement with a false antecedent does not tell us anything about the consequent. We cannot deduce \$C\$ from this premise alone.} \\
\texttt{*   Now look at **Premise 2** (\$ \textbackslash{}neg G \textbackslash{}to C \$). Since the person *is* a great{-}grandfather (\$G\$ is true), the antecedent (\$\textbackslash{}neg G\$) is false. Similarly, we cannot deduce \$C\$ from this premise.} \\
\\ 
\texttt{**The Logical Gap:**} \\
\texttt{The premises state that being a classmate is necessary for *not* being a workmate or *not* being a great{-}grandfather. However, the person in question *is* a workmate and *is* a great{-}grandfather. The premises do not state what happens if someone *is* a workmate and *is* a great{-}grandfather. It is logically possible for someone to be a cousin of Tony, be a workmate, be a great{-}grandfather, and *still not* be a classmate of Williams, without contradicting any of the premises.} \\
\\ 
\texttt{For the argument to be valid, the premises would need to establish that being a workmate and a great{-}grandfather implies being a classmate, or that the conditions for *not* being a classmate are mutually exclusive with being a workmate/great{-}grandfather. Since the premises only define the consequences of *not* being a workmate or *not* being a great{-}grandfather, they fail to force the conclusion for someone who *is* both.} \\
\\ 
\texttt{Therefore, the conclusion does not necessarily follow from the premises.} \\
\\
