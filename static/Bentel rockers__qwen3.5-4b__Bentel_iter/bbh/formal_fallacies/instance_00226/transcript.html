Is the argument, given the explicitly stated premises, deductively valid or invalid?
Options:
- valid
- invalid
A:

To determine the validity of this argument, let's break down the premises and the conclusion using logical notation.

**1. Analyze the Premises:**

* **Premise 1:** "Nobody is neither a classmate of Marisa nor a schoolmate of Karen."
* Let $C(x)$ = $x$ is a classmate of Marisa.
* Let $S(x)$ = $x$ is a schoolmate of Karen.
* "Neither $C$ nor $S$" translates to $\neg C(x) \land \neg S(x)$.
* "Nobody is [this]" means there is no $x$ such that $\neg C(x) \land \neg S(x)$.
* Logically, this is equivalent to saying: For all $x$, it is not the case that ($x$ is neither a classmate nor a schoolmate).
* $\forall x \neg (\neg C(x) \land \neg S(x))$
* By De Morgan's laws, this simplifies to: $\forall x (C(x) \lor S(x))$.
* **Meaning:** Everyone is either a classmate of Marisa or a schoolmate of Karen (or both).

* **Premise 2:** "Not every sister of Ruth is a schoolmate of Karen."
* Let $R(x)$ = $x$ is a sister of Ruth.
* "Not every $R$ is $S$" means there exists at least one $x$ such that $x$ is a sister of Ruth AND $x$ is NOT a schoolmate of Karen.
* $\exists x (R(x) \land \neg S(x))$.
* **Meaning:** There is at least one person who is a sister of Ruth but not a schoolmate of Karen.

**2. Analyze the Conclusion:**

* **Conclusion:** "Somebody is a sister of Ruth and not a classmate of Marisa."
* $\exists x (R(x) \land \neg C(x))$.
* **Meaning:** There is at least one person who is a sister of Ruth but not a classmate of Marisa.

**3. Evaluate the Logic:**

* From **Premise 2**, we know there exists a specific person (let's call them Person A) who is a **Sister of Ruth** ($R(A)$) and **Not a Schoolmate of Karen** ($\neg S(A)$).
* Now, apply **Premise 1** to Person A. Premise 1 states that for *everyone*, they must be either a Classmate of Marisa ($C$) or a Schoolmate of Karen ($S$).
* So, $C(A) \lor S(A)$ must be true.
* Since we know from Premise 2 that $S(A)$ is **false** ($\neg S(A)$), the only way for the disjunction ($C(A) \lor S(A)$) to be true is if $C(A)$ is **true**.
* Therefore, Person A must be a **Classmate of Marisa**.
* However, the conclusion claims that there is someone who is a **Sister of Ruth** AND **Not a Classmate of Marisa** ($\neg C$).
* This creates a contradiction. The premises force us to conclude that the person in question *is* a classmate of Marisa. The conclusion asserts they are *not*.
* Therefore, the conclusion does not follow from the premises. In fact, the premises imply the negation of the conclusion.

**Result:** The argument is **invalid**.
