                \texttt{To determine the validity of the argument, let's break down the logical structure using propositional logic.} \\
\\ 
\texttt{**1. Define the Propositions:**} \\
\texttt{Let \$F\$ = "is a close friend of Francine"} \\
\texttt{Let \$S\$ = "is a cousin of Samantha"} \\
\texttt{Let \$J\$ = "is an ancestor of Janice"} \\
\texttt{Let \$H\$ = "is a close friend of Shanika"} \\
\\ 
\texttt{**2. Translate the Premises:**} \\
\texttt{*   **Premise 1:** "Every close friend of Francine is a cousin of Samantha or an ancestor of Janice or a close friend of Shanika."} \\
\texttt{    *   Logic: \$F \textbackslash{}rightarrow (S \textbackslash{}lor J \textbackslash{}lor H)\$} \\
\texttt{*   **Premise 2:** "Every close friend of Francine is a cousin of Samantha."} \\
\texttt{    *   Logic: \$F \textbackslash{}rightarrow S\$} \\
\texttt{*   **Premise 3:** "Being a close friend of Shanika is necessary for being a close friend of Francine."} \\
\texttt{    *   Logic: \$F \textbackslash{}rightarrow H\$ (Note: "A is necessary for B" means \$B \textbackslash{}rightarrow A\$)} \\
\\ 
\texttt{**3. Translate the Conclusion:**} \\
\texttt{*   **Conclusion:** "Being an ancestor of Janice is necessary for being a close friend of Francine."} \\
\texttt{    *   Logic: \$F \textbackslash{}rightarrow J\$} \\
\\ 
\texttt{**4. Analyze the Validity:**} \\
\texttt{An argument is **deductively valid** if and only if it is impossible for the premises to be true and the conclusion false. In other words, the conclusion must follow necessarily from the premises.} \\
\\ 
\texttt{Let's test this with a counter{-}example (a scenario where premises are true, but the conclusion is false):} \\
\texttt{*   Assume there is a person \$x\$ who is a close friend of Francine (\$F\$ is true).} \\
\texttt{*   From Premise 2, \$x\$ must be a cousin of Samantha (\$S\$ is true).} \\
\texttt{*   From Premise 3, \$x\$ must be a close friend of Shanika (\$H\$ is true).} \\
\texttt{*   From Premise 1, since \$S\$ and \$H\$ are true, the disjunction \$(S \textbackslash{}lor J \textbackslash{}lor H)\$ is automatically true regardless of the truth value of \$J\$.} \\
\texttt{*   Now, consider the conclusion: Does \$x\$ *have* to be an ancestor of Janice (\$J\$)?} \\
\texttt{    *   The premises do not force \$J\$ to be true. \$x\$ could be a friend of Francine, a cousin of Samantha, and a friend of Shanika, but **not** an ancestor of Janice.} \\
\texttt{    *   If \$J\$ is false, the conclusion (\$F \textbackslash{}rightarrow J\$) becomes false (True \$\textbackslash{}rightarrow\$ False = False).} \\
\texttt{    *   However, all premises remain true in this scenario.} \\
\\ 
\texttt{Since we can construct a scenario where the premises are true and the conclusion is false, the argument is **invalid**. The fact that \$S\$ and \$H\$ are sufficient to satisfy the condition in Premise 1 means \$J\$ is not required.} \\
\\ 
\texttt{**Answer:**} \\
\texttt{invalid} \\
