Q: "Here comes a perfectly valid argument: First of all, nobody is neither a classmate of Marisa nor a schoolmate of Karen. Next, not every sister of Ruth is a schoolmate of Karen. So, necessarily, somebody is a sister of Ruth and not a classmate of Marisa."
Is the argument, given the explicitly stated premises, deductively valid or invalid?
Options:
- valid
- invalid
A:

To determine the validity of the argument, let's break it down into logical notation:

**Premise 1:** "Nobody is neither a classmate of Marisa nor a schoolmate of Karen."
* Let $C(x)$ be "x is a classmate of Marisa".
* Let $S(x)$ be "x is a schoolmate of Karen".
* "Neither $C$ nor $S$" translates to $\neg C(x) \land \neg S(x)$.
* "Nobody is..." means there does not exist an $x$ such that...
* Formalization: $\neg \exists x (\neg C(x) \land \neg S(x))$.
* By De Morgan's laws and quantifier negation, this is equivalent to: $\forall x (C(x) \lor S(x))$.
* **Meaning:** Everyone is either a classmate of Marisa or a schoolmate of Karen (or both).

**Premise 2:** "Not every sister of Ruth is a schoolmate of Karen."
* Let $R(x)$ be "x is a sister of Ruth".
* "Not every $R$ is $S$" means there exists at least one $x$ who is $R$ but not $S$.
* Formalization: $\exists x (R(x) \land \neg S(x))$.
* **Meaning:** There is at least one person who is a sister of Ruth and is NOT a schoolmate of Karen.

**Conclusion:** "Somebody is a sister of Ruth and not a classmate of Marisa."
* Formalization: $\exists x (R(x) \land \neg C(x))$.

**Logical Deduction:**
1. From Premise 2, we know there exists a specific individual, let's call them $a$, such that $R(a)$ is true and $S(a)$ is false ($R(a) \land \neg S(a)$).
2. From Premise 1, we know that for *everyone* (including $a$), $C(x) \lor S(x)$ is true. So, $C(a) \lor S(a)$ must be true.
3. Since we know $S(a)$ is false (from step 1), for the disjunction $C(a) \lor S(a)$ to be true, $C(a)$ must be true.
4. Therefore, for this individual $a$, we have:
* $R(a)$ is true (Sister of Ruth).
* $C(a)$ is true (Classmate of Marisa).
5. The conclusion claims there exists someone who is a sister of Ruth ($R$) AND **not** a classmate of Marisa ($\neg C$).
6. Our deduction shows that the person who is a sister of Ruth and not a schoolmate of Karen *must* be a classmate of Marisa.
7. The argument concludes that this person is *not* a classmate of Marisa. This contradicts the necessary result derived from the premises.

Wait, let me re-evaluate the conclusion structure.
The conclusion is: $\exists x (R(x) \land \neg C(x))$.
My deduction showed: For the specific $x$ that satisfies Premise 2 ($R(x) \land \neg S(x)$), it must be that $C(x)$ is true.
So, for that specific $x$, $R(x) \land \neg C(x)$ is **false**.

Does the existence of *another* person satisfy the conclusion?
The premises do not guarantee the existence of any other person.
Premise 2 guarantees at least one person ($a$) who is $R$ and $\neg S$.
Premise 1 forces that person ($a$) to be $C$.
So we have a person who is $R$ and $C$.
The conclusion requires a person who is $R$ and $\neg C$.
The premises do not provide any information that forces the existence of a person who is $R$ and $\neg C$. In fact, the premises are consistent with a world where *everyone* who is a sister of Ruth is a classmate of Marisa (specifically, the one sister we know of is a classmate).
If the premises are true, the conclusion *could* be false (if the only sister of Ruth is the one we identified, who is a classmate of Marisa).
Therefore, the conclusion does not necessarily follow.

Let's double check the logic.
P1: $\forall x (C(x) \lor S(x))$
P2: $\exists x (R(x) \land \neg S(x))$
Let $a$ be the witness for P2.
$R(a)$ is true.
$\neg S(a)$ is true.
From P1 applied to $a$: $C(a) \lor S(a)$. Since $\neg S(a)$, then $C(a)$ must be true.
So we have established: $R(a) \land C(a)$.
The Conclusion claims: $\exists x (R(x) \land \neg C(x))$.
Is it possible that $R(a) \land C(a)$ is true, but no one satisfies $R(x) \land \neg C(x)$?
Yes. Imagine a world with only one person, $a$.
$a$ is a sister of Ruth.
$a$ is not a schoolmate of Karen.
$a$ is a classmate of Marisa.
P1: Everyone is $C$ or $S$. ($a$ is $C$, so True).
P2: Not every sister is $S$. ($a$ is sister and not $S$, so True).
Conclusion: Someone is sister and not $C$. ($a$ is sister and IS $C$. No one else exists. So False).
Since we found a scenario where premises are true and conclusion is false, the argument is **invalid**.
