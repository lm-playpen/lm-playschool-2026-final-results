\texttt{If we use **Method 1** (adjusting for smoking), we are controlling for the mediator (Smoking). While this can sometimes reduce bias by blocking the backdoor path from Gene to Lung Cancer via Smoking, it does not fully eliminate bias if there are other unobserved confounders or if the relationship between Gene and Smoking is not perfectly linear or if there are interactions. \texttt{However, the critical flaw in Method 1 is that **Smoking is unobserved**. In causal diagrams, if a variable is unobserved, we cannot adjust for it directly. We can only adjust for observed variables. If we look at the correlation between Gene and Lung Cancer *conditioned on* Smoking, we are essentially looking at the association between Gene and Lung Cancer *given* that Smoking occurred. If the effect of Gene on Smoking is positive (more genes lead to more smoking), and the effect of Smoking on Lung Cancer is positive, then the observed correlation between Gene and Lung Cancer will be inflated by the confounding effect of Smoking. Even if we condition on Smoking, the bias introduced by the unobserved nature of Smoking (specifically, the fact that we don't know the distribution of Smoking across the population) remains problematic unless we have a valid instrumental variable or a specific structural assumption that perfectly isolates the causal path.} \\
